# WareOrb pitch and recording guide

The approved direction is the complete capacity-reallocation journey. Jarvis and the 3D warehouse support that journey. Target **6:45**, including the introduction, a **2:50** separately recorded demo and transitions. The remaining 15 seconds are a safety margin, not extra planned material. Delivery speed must be checked by a human read-through.

## Feature priorities

| Priority | Feature | What the judge should understand |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Capacity reallocation | Ageing stock receives sales attention, a buyer offer becomes a costed proposal, the owner approves, and confirmed collection releases physical space. |
| 2 | Jarvis | An interface to stock information and role-appropriate work. It helps staff act on the records. It does not replace the owner or warehouse verifier. |
| 3 | 3D warehouse | Physical context and visible state: occupied, reserved and genuinely released bays. No claim of a validated geometric packing optimiser. |
| Foundation | Receiving and stock records | Draft entry and human verification create trustworthy inputs. Keep this short in the video. |
| Follow-through | Replenishment advice | Recorded sales and a transparent coverage rule suggest quantities, constrained by space and budget. The owner reviews purchases. |
| Supporting controls | Roles, versioned approval and history | Demonstrate these inside the journey. Avoid a separate feature catalogue. |
| Outside the main pitch | Automatic buyer discovery, production optimisation, payments, advanced forecasting | Not established by the reviewed build. Do not imply these are working. |

## Why this order

Three plausible approaches were considered: a feature tour, a financial-comparison pitch, and a complete operating journey. The chosen journey makes capacity reallocation memorable and gives Jarvis and 3D a specific purpose. A feature tour would dilute the main feature. Leading with the old financial comparison would overemphasise a keep-versus-clear calculator that is narrower than the newer product direction.

The old pitch's RM400 improvement and RM200 monthly-cost illustration are hypothetical. The current demo uses a different RM900 recovery calculation. The deck deliberately uses the current demo case and keeps monthly adoption cost as a separate, clearly marked assumption.

## Speaking script

### Slide 1: WareOrb (0:00–0:20)

A furniture warehouse can be full while the business still needs different stock. Older sofas occupy useful space, and the next replenishment has to wait. We’re building WareOrb to help small merchants act on that problem, with the owner in control.

### Slide 2: Slow stock needs a next step (0:20–0:55)

Here is our example: four older sofas occupy two bays. The batch is one hundred and twenty days old, with no sales of that model in the last thirty days. Those facts justify attention, not an automatic clearance. Someone still needs to find a buyer, check delivery costs and arrange collection. Until the goods actually leave, that space remains occupied. WareOrb connects those steps so the stock record leads to a decision and a visible result.

### Slide 3: Capacity reallocation (0:55–1:40)

Capacity reallocation is our main feature. WareOrb identifies a batch needing attention and prepares a brief for the salesperson. The salesperson finds a buyer and records the offer and delivery requirements. WareOrb combines those requirements with the cargo information, compares complete transport quotes and prepares the owner's decision. Approval reserves the goods. Warehouse staff then confirm collection, which updates stock, frees the actual bay and records the action. The updated records also support replenishment advice. This is one connected operating journey, rather than several disconnected tasks.

### Slide 4: Jarvis and 3D make the journey usable (1:40–2:10)

Jarvis is the entry point: staff can ask about stock and open the next task in ordinary language. The 3D view connects that information to physical locations. It makes the difference between occupied, reserved and released space easier to understand. Receiving supplies the underlying records, with people checking the goods before posting stock. AI can help interpret inputs, while explicit rules calculate quantities and costs. Let’s follow one batch through the prototype.

### Slide 5: One batch. One complete journey. (2:10–5:00)

Transition directly into the separate 2 minute 50 second screen recording. This title is only a short transition inside that allocation. Use the detailed demo script in the companion recording guide.

### Slide 6: Cash recovered and space released (5:00–5:50)

In the demo, a buyer offers twelve hundred ringgit in total for two sofas. Delivery costs two hundred and eighty, and packing costs twenty, leaving nine hundred ringgit recovered before the original stock cost. That is not nine hundred ringgit of profit, or a saving created entirely by WareOrb. The business case is the additional benefit over the merchant's existing process: fewer avoidable delivery costs, less coordination and usable space for stock with demonstrated sales. If total adoption cost were two hundred ringgit a month, the merchant would need more than that in additional monthly benefit. Our next test is to measure that benefit against spreadsheets and normal messaging.

### Slide 7: Furniture SMEs first (5:50–6:25)

We start with small furniture merchants and a workflow they can repeat across batches and staff. Existing spreadsheets or point-of-sale records can remain inputs. WareOrb adds the follow-through between records, approvals and physical stock. That fits Orbyte's shared business-hub approach, although our Orbyte connection is proposed. The main risk is unreliable input data, so the design makes missing costs visible and requires human confirmation. A pilot would measure decision time, avoidable costs and stock accuracy, supporting our intended contribution to SDG eight point two.

### Slide 8: Make room for the next proven seller (6:25–6:45)

Our central promise is simple: help a merchant turn ageing stock into an approved sale, a confirmed collection and space they can actually use. Jarvis guides the work, and the 3D warehouse makes the result visible. That is WareOrb.

## Recording setup and constraints

- Use a prepared synthetic scenario. Set the buyer offer to **RM1,200 total for TWO sofas**, not RM1,200 per sofa. The handoff file's “RM1,200 × 2” wording conflicts with the detailed scenario and RM900 arithmetic.
- Set packing to RM20. A new deal starts with zero packing in the reviewed code, so the speaker cannot assume that field is already filled.
- Preserve the distinction between the newly received chairs and the older sofa batch. Receiving now does not make that stock 120 days old.
- Demo the synthetic carrier replies explicitly. A saved API-fallback quote is a different category and is not ranked or approvable in the reviewed cost rules.
- Confirm the buyer destination and vehicle fit before submitting for approval. A simple quote price does not prove a vehicle fits the cargo.
- Keep the visible booking label “simulated.” No real carrier enquiry, booking or payment is needed.
- The four-unit stock count in the script refers to the selected old batch. Total stock for that SKU includes another batch and may be eight. Frame the batch detail so the narration matches the display.
- Stop the replenishment segment at the suggestion if ordering would make the demo too long. A purchase order is incoming stock until receipt.
- Use typed Jarvis queries or commands for predictable timing. Demonstrate voice only if it works reliably in the actual recording environment. Do not represent an offline command match as a live model response.
- Cut waiting and repetitive typing. Keep approval, collection and stock-state changes visible so the sequence is understandable. Do not use edits to suggest an unimplemented action worked.
- The local source code was reviewed. The deployed application and service credentials were not tested for this pitch task. Rehearse the exact path on the build you will submit.

## Visual and caption direction

- Eight widescreen slides. Large type, dark navy and off-white, with teal emphasis. No generated art, decorative stock images or invented application screens.
- One argument per slide. Speaker notes carry the explanation and source detail.
- For the recorded video, add speech captions in the editor, usually no more than two lines at once. Keep them below the important app content and away from approval controls.

## Mentor advice translated into pitch decisions

| Mentor point | Response in this pitch |
|---|---|
| What does “low” or “too much” actually mean? | Show age, a stated sales window and occupied bays. Explain replenishment with a visible coverage calculation. |
| Why is the feature worth paying for? | Separate recovered cash from incremental value. Compare realised benefit with total adoption cost. |
| Do not sell AI merely because it looks impressive | Put the operating journey first, then show why Jarvis and the 3D view help users complete it. |
| Reasonable assumptions are acceptable if their reasoning is clear | Label synthetic records, cost assumptions and unmeasured benefits. Do not turn illustrative figures into validated ROI. |
| Explain cost and revenue effects | Discuss avoidable delivery costs, coordination effort and the opportunity to replenish sellers, without asserting measured sales uplift. |

The mentor mentions a Poisson distribution as an example around 03:22–03:46. The inspected build uses transparent ageing and coverage rules. Do not claim Poisson forecasting, seasonality modelling or validated demand prediction.

## Business-value interpretation

The current synthetic sale calculation is:

**RM1,200 total proceeds − RM280 merchant-paid delivery − RM20 packing = RM900 net recovery before original stock cost.**

This recovery is not profit, not incremental improvement against keeping stock, and not proof WareOrb caused the sale. Physical space becomes available only after collection. A freed bay does not automatically reduce fixed rent.

The monthly RM200 figure is an **illustrative total adoption-cost threshold**, inherited from the existing pitch, not agreed subscription pricing. Include software, setup allowance and record maintenance when testing it. A merchant would need more than RM200 of attributable additional monthly benefit to exceed that assumed cost. Count avoided expenses or additional contribution only when supported; track staff time freed separately unless it changes an actual expense or productive output.

A pilot should compare the same kinds of cases with a competent spreadsheet-and-messaging process. Measure coordination minutes, re-entered fields, quote issues caught, realised sale/transport outcomes and stock accuracy. No pilot outcomes were available in the inspected sources.

## Build status and claim limits

The newer app folder contains receiving, ageing, salesperson handoff, quotation handling, owner approval, collection and replenishment code. This is stronger evidence of implementation than the older deck's “in development” labels, but source presence is not proof that the submitted deployment works.

The handoff reports that offline paths were checked and model/voice/provider-key paths remained pending at the time it was written. Do not claim live Lalamove, model-backed Jarvis or live Orbyte integration without evidence from the actual build used in the recording. Simulated roles are allowed by the supplied Domain 2 brief.

The current replenishment calculation uses net sales over a window and a configurable coverage target, available stock, incoming stock, free bays and budget. It does not directly model supplier lead time or fit a statistical forecast. Describe lead time as an owner review consideration unless the submitted build adds that logic.

For production, propose role-restricted records, minimum necessary shipment sharing and a defined retention policy. This is a privacy design direction, not a claim of certified compliance.
